Cap Rate Explained

The most important metric in real estate investing — formula, NOI, market benchmarks and how it differs from cash-on-cash return.

NOI formulaMarket benchmarksCap rate vs CoCUpdated 2026

What is cap rate?

Cap rate (capitalization rate) is the most widely used metric in real estate investing. It measures a property's annual income yield as a percentage of its price — completely ignoring financing. A 6% cap rate means you earn 6 cents of net operating income for every dollar of purchase price.

Cap Rate Formula
Net Operating Income (NOI)Annual rent − Operating expenses
Cap RateNOI ÷ Purchase Price × 100%
Or: Purchase PriceNOI ÷ Cap Rate

What counts as NOI?

Net Operating Income = Gross rental income − Vacancy allowance − Operating expenses. Operating expenses include property taxes, insurance, property management, maintenance, and owner-paid utilities. NOI does not include mortgage payments — that's what makes cap rate financing-agnostic and useful for comparing properties regardless of how they're purchased.

Example — $500,000 Rental Property
Gross annual rent$42,000
Vacancy (5%)−$2,100
Property management (10%)−$3,990
Property tax + insurance + maintenance−$7,200
NOI$28,710
Cap Rate5.74%

Key takeaway

Cap rate is the best single metric for comparing investment properties on equal footing. Use it to screen and compare — then use cash-on-cash return to evaluate the actual leveraged return on your capital.

Frequently asked questions

Can cap rate be negative?
Technically yes — if operating expenses exceed gross income. In practice, a negative cap rate means the property loses money at the operating level. For rental income properties, a negative cap rate is a red flag.
Should I include mortgage payments in the cap rate calculation?
No — cap rate deliberately excludes financing. If you want to include your mortgage, use cash-on-cash return instead (NOI minus debt service, divided by cash invested). The Rental ROI calculator calculates both.
What's the 1% rule and how does it relate to cap rate?
The 1% rule says monthly rent should be at least 1% of the purchase price — a gross yield of 12% annually. After expenses, that typically yields a cap rate of 7–8%. In most major US cities, the 1% rule is nearly impossible to find.
How do interest rates affect cap rates?
When borrowing costs rise, investors demand higher cap rates — pushing property prices down. The spread between cap rates and the 10-year Treasury yield is a key metric: below 1% signals an overheated market; above 2–3% indicates a more attractive entry point.
